AI adoption that showed up in the hiring plan

The CEO made AI a quarterly company objective and gave it to me. Thirty people, almost none of whom had used these tools. A quarter later the program had saved 200+ hours, and two of the three open hiring requests had quietly disappeared. One department never moved. This page is the playbook, and the model I built from that failure.

Starting point

Enthusiasm was not the problem

Why it existed

An earlier automation had removed a 4-hour daily task and a planned hire. With new agentic tools on the market, the CEO set company-wide AI adoption as a quarterly objective, with me as owner.

The baseline that mattered

Not "how many people use AI". There were three open hiring requests in three departments. If the program worked, it should show up there, and nobody would need to report it.

The real constraint

95% of the company had never used these tools, in a regulated business where mistakes have consequences. A demo and a login were not going to change how anyone worked.

The playbook

Five steps, one quarter

Inventory

Each team lists its recurring activities and the hours per week each one takes. Their numbers, not mine.

Score

Business impact and how automatable each activity is. Output: a prioritized project list per department.

Read the room

Before each 1:1, I analyzed the team's message history: who asks whom for what, and how long it takes. I arrived with hypotheses and prompts written for that person's real work.

Familiarity, not automation

Sessions designed so leads would invent their own uses, not depend on me. Then two days working alongside each team, and a check-in two weeks later.

Monitor and rebalance

Track usage by department. When teams hit their usage limits, that's a good problem: help them make the workflow efficient instead of buying more.

What didn't work

The department that didn't move

Same process, same sessions, same support. Looking back, every warning sign was visible in the first month:

Week 1

The activity inventory arrived late, as a list of names with no hours and no criteria.

Inventory late · no numbers
Week 2

The lead joined the sessions remotely while every other lead came in person, and said openly that AI felt uncomfortable and that this had to change.

Remote only
Weeks 3–6

Whole weeks with no usage, while peers already had the tools in their daily flow. The day-to-day load always won.

Lead not using the tools
Months 2–3

I escalated: a full-stack engineer and an external advisor joined, and a team member who did engage helped us map several steps. I ended a three-month external engagement once it was clear it would produce nothing implemented.

One hands-on champion

"I assessed how automatable each process was. I never assessed whether the team that had to adopt it was ready. I diagnosed the work, not the people."

My root cause

The second-order reading came later: the program failed as an implementation in that department, but it worked as a diagnostic. The resistance turned out to be a symptom of a team problem that already existed and that nobody had named. That doesn't excuse the miss. It's what I now look for first.

What I'd carry to any company

Principles

Pick the metric before the tool

Open hiring requests were the baseline. Adoption that matters shows up in the plan, not in a usage dashboard.

Prepare with their work

Reading the message history first meant every session started from a real pain, with a prompt already written for it.

Teach familiarity, not automations

The goal of the session was for people to invent their own uses. The teams that did kept going without me.

Score the team, not just the task

Automatability tells you what is possible. Readiness tells you what will actually happen. Measure both in week one.
